Why Church Bookkeeping Matters

Before counting a single cedi, it helps to know why you’re counting.

Your win today: You can explain to any elder or member why accurate records are an act of stewardship, and why church bookkeeping is different from keeping a shop’s cash book.
Concepts: C1, C2 ~15 minutes Quiz: 5 questions Module 1 of 7

The Deacon’s Question

When the early church in Jerusalem found that some widows were being overlooked in the daily distribution of food, the apostles did something interesting. They did not simply fix the problem themselves — they appointed seven men of good reputation, full of the Spirit and wisdom, to manage the practical and financial affairs of the community. [1]

That is the origin of the deacon’s financial role. The congregation brings its gifts in faith, trusting that they will reach the right purpose. Your job as the keeper of those records is not primarily a technical one — it is a trust. The ledger is where that trust is either honoured or broken.

So before we talk about debits and credits, let us be clear about why any of this matters.

Three Reasons Church Records Matter

1

Stewardship

The money is not yours, the pastor’s, or the elders’. It belongs to the congregation’s mission. Your records prove it was used that way.

2

Accountability

The elders cannot lead wisely without accurate numbers. Members cannot give in faith without trusting that the numbers are real.

3

Protection

A missing receipt or unrecorded expense leaves you exposed to accusation. Good records protect the innocent — including the bookkeeper.

A study of financial accountability in Ghanaian churches found that the most common weaknesses were blind trust (relying on individuals without systems), limited financial literacy, and weak receipt controls — and that these weaknesses eroded community trust over time. [2] Systems, not character alone, are what protect a church and its people.

Church Accounting vs. a Shop’s Cash Book

You already keep a basic cash book — income in, expenses out. That is exactly how a small shop keeps its records too. But a church and a shop have different goals, and those different goals change how the books must be kept. [3]

A shop’s goal
  • Income − Expenses = Profit
  • Profit goes to the owner
  • All money is interchangeable
  • Success = surplus to the owner
A church’s goal
  • Income − Expenses = Surplus
  • Surplus returns to the mission
  • Money is designated by purpose
  • Success = faithful use of gifts

The biggest difference is the phrase designated by purpose. When a member gives GHS 200 to the Building Fund, that money can only be spent on the building. It cannot quietly become next month’s electricity payment, even if the church is short. The donor trusted that their gift would go to the building — and the records must show that it did. [4]

This is called fund accounting — the practice of separating money into distinct pools (funds) based on their intended purpose. We will go deep into it in Module 2. For now, just hold the idea: in a church, not all money is the same.

Your Cash Book — and What It Cannot Do

The cash book you are already using is genuinely useful. You know which Sunday brought in how much, and you can see what went out last month. That foundation is worth building on.

But a single-column cash book has three limits that will matter:

Limit 1 — No second check
A cash book records each transaction once. An error — a wrong figure, a forgotten entry — can sit undetected for months. Double-entry bookkeeping (the method this course teaches) records each transaction twice, in a way that forces the books to balance. Errors are caught before they compound.
Limit 2 — Funds are invisible
Your cash book probably shows one running total. If GHS 3,000 came in last month — how much went to the General Fund, how much to the Building Fund, how much to Missions? A single-column cash book cannot answer that question cleanly.
Limit 3 — No picture of what the church owns
The cash book shows what happened this month. It cannot tell you: what is in the bank account right now? What does the church owe? What does it own? That requires a balance sheet — which we build in Module 5.

This course will teach you to keep records that answer all three questions. You will still write down income and expenses — the cash book instinct is correct — but you will add the second entry that keeps everything balanced, and the fund columns that keep designated money safe.
