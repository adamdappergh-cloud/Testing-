Form-Making  ·  Lesson 1

Climate as the First Move

Turning sun, wind and rain into the start of a form

The Space Planning series taught you to read and organise a plan. This series is about the building's form — its three-dimensional shape — and it's hands-on: you'll generate form ideas, not just judge them. We start somewhere specific. In a hot climate, the most honest place a form can come from is the climate itself. Before any shape, you map the forces on your Pokuase site — where the sun goes, where the breeze comes from, where the rain falls — and let them push the form into being. By the end you'll have made your first artefact: a site-force map. (We're deliberately setting the existing concept plan aside — this is exploration.)

Tier 1 · Core Four forces, four form-moves Each fact of the climate is the start of a shape.

A form isn't a style you pick — in this approach it's the visible answer to a set of forces. For Pokuase (~6°N), four forces dominate, and each one suggests a clear opening move:

The force (Pokuase)What it pushes the form to do
Sun — high overhead; hot, low sun at east & west (west worst)A broad shading roof with deep overhangs; turn the short faces to east/west (long axis roughly E–W) so little wall takes the low sun; screen or buffer the west.
Breeze — the SW monsoon, most of the yearOpen and face the form to the south-west; keep it thin or porous (and maybe raised) so air passes through rather than around.
Rain — heavy seasonal downpoursBig pitched, overhanging roofs; a raised floor / plinth; covered links between parts; somewhere for the water to go.
Heat & humidityShade everything; let air move: tall rooms that vent at the top (hot air rises out), lightweight shaded walls, generous openings.

Notice these forces sometimes agree (a big roof answers both sun and rain) and sometimes pull against each other (you want to open to the SW breeze, but the low afternoon sun also comes from the west). Form-making is the act of resolving those pulls into one shape. That tension is the work — and the fun.

A site-force map: draw the forces onto the plot, and the first form-moves write themselves. This is the artefact you'll make at the end of the lesson.

Your Core win. You can now look at a bare site and name the moves the climate is asking for, before any room is drawn. That alone reframes the whole design: form isn't decoration added at the end — it's the climate answer made visible.

You can stop here and already be ahead. Four forces, four moves, and the habit of drawing them on the site is enough to start generating shapes. When you want to see why the masters trust climate to generate form — and how they built whole careers on it — read on.
Tier 2 · Deeper Form follows forces Olgyay, Fathy and Murcutt: in a hot climate, the climate is a legitimate generator of form.

You've heard "form follows function" (Louis Sullivan). In a hot climate there's an older, deeper driver: form follows forces — the sun, the wind, the rain. Three masters make the case.

Design with climate (Olgyay)

Victor Olgyay's Design with Climate (1963) founded the bioclimatic approach: the building's shape and orientation are the first and cheapest environmental control you have — long before insulation, glazing or machinery. Get the form right and you do most of the cooling for free. Get it wrong and you spend the building's life fighting the climate with energy.

The vernacular already solved it (Fathy)

Hassan Fathy looked at hot-climate villages and saw that their forms are their climate answers: the courtyard, the wind-catcher (malqaf), the thick shaded wall, the domed roof. None of it is decoration — every shape does a thermal job. The lesson for you: you're not inventing from nothing; you're rejoining a long tradition of letting climate shape the building.

Touch this earth lightly (Murcutt)

The Australian master Glenn Murcutt — who works alone, by hand — tunes every house to the sun, wind and rain of its exact site, summed up in his motto "touch this earth lightly." His houses are long, thin, roof-led and full of adjustable openings: pure climate-form. He's proof that this method produces not just comfortable buildings but beautiful, distinctive ones.

The sun does not realise how wonderful it is until after a room is made.
— Louis Kahn (the spirit of the whole series)

Tier 3 · Apply Make your site-force map The first artefact of the series.

Hands-on. On a single sheet, draw your Pokuase plot as a simple outline (a rectangle is fine for now) and lay the forces over it:

  1. North — mark it, then draw the sun's arc (rises E, high overhead, sets W) and shade the hot west edge.
  2. Breeze — a fat arrow from the south-west.
  3. Rain — note it over the top; and any slope / views / road on your actual plot.
  4. Beside each force, write the form-move it asks for (from the table above).
  5. Finally, circle the one tension you'll have to resolve — almost always: open to the SW breeze, but defend against the west sun.
What you've made: a one-page site-force map — the generative seed for every shape we explore next. Keep it; Lesson 2 turns the first and biggest move (the roof) into real form options.
Primary sources for this lesson Victor Olgyay, Design with Climate (the bioclimatic method); Hassan Fathy, natural-energy vernacular; Glenn Murcutt, "touch this earth lightly" (Pritzker, 2002).
