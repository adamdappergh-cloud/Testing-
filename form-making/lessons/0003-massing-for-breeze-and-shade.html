Form-Making  ·  Lesson 3

Massing for Breeze and Shade

Shaping the bulk under the roof so air moves through and the building shades itself

You have a lead roof from Lesson 2. Now we grow the massing beneath it — the overall arrangement and bulk of the building. In the tropics, massing answers two desires at once: let the breeze through, and make the building shade itself. A fat, sealed block fails both. This lesson explores the massing moves that succeed, and by the end you'll have made your third artefact: three massing options for the Pokuase site. (Still generating freely from the forces — no concept plan.)

Tier 1 · Core Thin, broken, and lifted Keep it thin for breeze; break and lift it for air and shade.

A compact, deep block is the worst tropical massing: air can't cross it and heat sits in its core. The winning moves are the opposite — and each is a shape you can draw:

Massing moveWhat it does for the climate
Thin bar (one room deep)Cross-ventilation: the SW breeze crosses straight through, and every room gets air and daylight from two sides. Run the long axis roughly E–W so the short faces meet the low east/west sun.
Pavilions / fingers with gapsBreak the bulk into smaller parts so air flows between them and they shade each other; the gaps become breezeways and courtyards.
Lifted on stilts / a plinthAir moves under the floor; flood, damp and ground heat are kept out; the breeze reaches the underside.
Courtyard / void (carved)A self-shading void the building breathes inward to — cooler, sheltered, drawing air through the rooms around it.
Verandas / wraps (added)Shade the walls and openings, throw the sun off, and make outdoor rooms on the breeze side.

Two of these are additive (you join parts — wings, pavilions, verandas) and two are subtractive (you carve the volume — courtyards, light-wells, notches). Massing is simply deciding what to add and what to carve so the result breathes and shades itself. Watch the contrast:

Two massings in plan (north up, so west is on the left). Left: a compact, deep block — the breeze slides around it and heat sits in the core. Right: thin bars with a carved courtyard and a west veranda — air passes between and through, and the parts shade each other.

Your Core win. You can now shape the building's bulk on purpose: thin for breeze, broken for air and mutual shade, lifted off the wet ground, carved or wrapped to shade itself. That is massing as a climate act, not just a footprint.

Tier 2 · Deeper Add, subtract, and let it breathe Ching's grammar of form in service of the courtyard and the pavilion.

Massing has a neutral vocabulary and a tropical purpose. Hold both.

The grammar (Ching)

Francis D. K. Ching, in Architecture: Form, Space & Order, names the two ways to make form. Additive form joins parts — in a line, a cluster, around a centre. Subtractive form carves a volume away. Every massing you draw is some mix of the two: you add pavilions, wings and verandas, and you subtract courtyards, light-wells and notches. That's the whole grammar.

The courtyard — the great carve (Fathy & the vernacular)

The courtyard is the classic subtractive move of hot-climate architecture. Hassan Fathy showed how a carved central void shades itself, collects cool night air, and lets the house breathe inward away from the harsh outside. It needs no view to work — the void is the amenity.

The pavilion — the great addition (Bawa, Murcutt, vernacular)

The opposite instinct: break the program into thin, separate pavilions linked by breezeways and verandas, so each catches air and they shade one another. Bawa's and Murcutt's houses are often less "a building" than a loose cluster of roofed parts with the climate moving between them.

Close to home — the Akan compound

You don't have to look far for the courtyard. The traditional Akan / Asante compound house of southern Ghana is exactly this: rooms (and roofed porches) arranged around an open central court that organises life and lets the building breathe. It is local, climate-tested precedent for your own Pokuase massing.

Architecture is the masterly, correct and magnificent play of masses brought together in light.
— Le Corbusier, Towards a New Architecture

Tier 3 · Apply Make your massing options Your third artefact — three massings under your lead roof.

Hands-on. Take your lead roof (Lesson 2) and your site-force map. Sketch the building's bulk three ways — a rough plan plus a quick 3D (axonometric) for each — using a different strategy:

  1. Thin bar — one room deep, long axis E–W, under a single sheltering roof; the breeze crosses straight through.
  2. Pavilions + breezeway — break the program into two or three thin parts with gaps and covered links; air flows between, and they shade each other.
  3. Courtyard — wrap the rooms around a carved central court; the void shades and breathes (your Akan-compound option).

On each, draw the forces: the SW breeze (does it pass through, not around?), the sun on the E/W short faces (is the west shaded by a veranda?), and lift it on a plinth or stilts. Note for each which forces it serves best and what it costs (a courtyard needs more enclosure; a long thin bar eats site length; pavilions multiply roofs).

Then circle the massing that best carries your lead roof and resolves the Lesson 1 tension — open to the SW breeze, defend the west sun, shed the rain, breathe out the heat.

What you've made: three massing options — the building's bulk explored three ways, each tested for breeze, shade and damp rather than for looks. Keep your strongest; Lesson 4 dissolves its edges with verandas, screens and courtyards — the gradient between inside and out.
