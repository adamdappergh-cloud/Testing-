Form-Making  ·  Lesson 2

The Roof as Primary Form

In the tropics, the roof is the building — shade, shelter and breath in one shape

In Lesson 1 you made a site-force map. Look at it: the biggest, first move it asks for is almost always the roof. In a hot, wet climate the roof is not a lid you add at the end — it is the primary form, the single element that does the most climate work. So we tackle it first. This lesson turns the roof into real, comparable form options, and by the end you'll have made your second artefact: three roof studies in section. (Still exploring freely from the site — no concept plan in sight.)

Tier 1 · Core The roof does three jobs Shade, shed, breathe — and its shape is the answer to all three.

On the Pokuase site the roof meets three forces at once. Each one pushes its shape:

The jobWhat it pushes the roof to do
Shade (sun)Deep overhangs / wide eaves (think ~1 m or more) and a broad, spreading form — even a "parasol" that floats over everything — so the walls and openings sit in shadow all day.
Shed (rain)A generous pitch plus those overhangs throw the heavy water clear of the walls and the raised plinth, so nothing splashes back or rots the base.
Breathe (heat)A vented or raised ridge, a double (ventilated) roof, or a tall internal volume lets hot air escape at the top — the stack effect — pulling cooler air in low.

Because one shape must do all three, the roof becomes the dominant gesture of a tropical building — often larger than the walls, reaching well past them, and lifted to breathe. When you generate roofs, work from four families:

  • The broad pitched / hip roof with deep eaves — the reliable workhorse.
  • The parasol — one large roof detached and floating above the rooms, shading the whole thing, with an air gap that vents.
  • The double / ventilated roof — a second skin or raised ridge whose job is to let heat out.
  • The twin-ridge / butterfly — to steer breeze, light or water (use with care where rain is heavy).

The roof is also where Lesson 1's tensions get resolved: the same overhang that defends the wall from the west sun can frame the opening you turn to the SW breeze, and the same pitch that sheds the rain can be lifted at the ridge to vent the heat. One form, doing several jobs at once — that is good tropical form-making.

A tropical section: the roof is the primary form. A broad parasol with deep overhangs shades the walls, a pitch throws the rain clear of a raised plinth, and a vented ridge lets hot air out while the breeze enters low. Three climate jobs, one big shape.

Tier 2 · Deeper The roof as the whole idea Bawa, Fathy and Murcutt built their architecture around the roof.

For these three masters the roof isn't one element among many — it's the move the whole building hangs from.

Bawa — the sheltering roof (Sri Lanka)

Geoffrey Bawa's tropical modernism makes the broad, deep-eaved roof the defining gesture. Under it the walls relax into verandas, loggias and shaded courtyards: you live under the roof as much as inside the walls. The roof manufactures cool, in-between outdoor rooms — exactly the spaces a hot-humid climate wants.

Fathy — the roof that vents heat (Egypt)

Hassan Fathy's domes and vaults are roofs working as thermal devices. Their height and curved surface let the hottest air collect and escape high above the occupants, and — paired with low openings — they drive air through the room. The shape itself does the cooling; nothing is bolted on.

Murcutt — the roof that reads sun and wind (Australia)

Glenn Murcutt's curved, winged metal roofs are tuned like an instrument: they scoop the breeze and vent heat at a raised ridge or "letterbox" slot, while long eaves track the sun across the day. The section — the cut through the roof — carries almost the whole design.

(The floating parasol roof has a modernist pedigree too — Le Corbusier used a giant umbrella roof for shade in his Indian work. We'll pick up the screens and brise-soleil under these roofs in Lesson 4.)

In the wet tropics you don't put a roof on a house — you put a house under a roof.
— the tropical-modern principle (the spirit of Bawa, Fathy and Murcutt)

Tier 3 · Apply Make your roof studies Your second artefact — three roofs in section.

Hands-on. Take your Lesson 1 site-force map. On a single sheet, draw your plot in section (a vertical cut through it) three times, and put a different roof on each:

  1. Broad pitched roof, deep eaves — the workhorse. Push the eaves ~1 m or more past the walls.
  2. Parasol — one big roof floating above the rooms with an air gap; let the gap breathe.
  3. Double / vented ridge — raise or split the ridge so hot air escapes at the very top.

On each section, draw the three forces exactly as in the diagram: a sun ray meeting the eave (does the wall fall into shade?), a rain line (does the water land clear of the plinth?), and the hot-air path (where does it leave?). Add the SW breeze entering low. Under each, write one line: which force it handles best, and where it struggles.

Then circle the option that best resolves your Lesson 1 tension — open to the SW breeze, defend the west sun, shed the rain. That's your lead roof going forward.

What you've made: three roof studies in section — real, comparable form options, each judged by sun, rain and air rather than by looks. Keep your favourite; Lesson 3 grows the volumes beneath it into massing.
