Form-Making  ·  Lesson 4

Dissolving Inside and Out

Turning the wall into a gradient of enclosure — veranda, screen, courtyard

You have a massing from Lesson 3. Now we work its edge. In a cool climate the wall is a hard line: warm in, cold out, sealed tight. In the hot-humid tropics that line is the wrong idea — you want the air, the shade and the outdoors close. So the edge dissolves into a gradient: garden → veranda → screen → room → courtyard, each layer doing a climate job, and the best rooms are often the in-between ones. By the end you'll have made your fourth artefact: your building's edge drawn as a gradient of enclosure.

Tier 1 · Core The wall becomes a gradient Not inside-or-outside, but a series of half-steps you live in.

Replace the single wall with a sequence of thresholds. Each layer filters the climate a little more, and each is a form you can draw:

Edge layerWhat it does for the climate
Veranda / loggiaA roofed but open edge-room: shades the wall, catches the breeze, sheds rain off the glass, and becomes the most-used room — outdoor living in the shade.
Screen / brise-soleil (jali, mashrabiya, louvres)A permeable skin: cuts the sun and gives privacy while still passing air and filtered light. A wall you can see and breathe through.
Breezeway / covered linkA roofed open passage that channels the breeze between parts and connects pavilions in shade.
Operable edge (shutters, louvres, sliding/folding walls)Lets you tune the openness hour by hour and season by season — wide open to the breeze, shut against the storm or the Harmattan.
Courtyard / gardenThe outside drawn in: a shaded, breathing void the rooms open onto from the protected side.

Read across a tropical section and you don't cross a wall — you pass through layers, from open to filtered to enclosed and back to open. That is form as a gradient of enclosure:

A tropical edge in section: you don't cross a wall, you move through layers. The deep eave catches the sun and the screen filters what's left, while the breeze passes through every layer — inside and outside dissolve into a gradient.

Your Core win. You can now design the edge as a sequence — veranda, screen, operable wall, court — instead of a single line. That alone unlocks the most comfortable, most loved spaces in a tropical building: the shaded in-between rooms.

You can stop here and already be ahead. The gradient — open, roofed-open, filtered, enclosed — is enough to transform any edge. When you want to see the masters who built whole architectures on the blurred edge, read on.
Tier 2 · Deeper Masters of the blurred edge Bawa's veranda, Le Corbusier's brise-soleil, and the screen the vernacular perfected.

Three traditions made the dissolved edge into architecture.

Bawa — you are never sure if you're in or out (Sri Lanka)

Geoffrey Bawa's houses are a promenade through thresholds: courtyards, verandas and loggias chained together so you move continuously between sheltered and open, shaded and bright. The "rooms" you remember are usually the half-open ones. The edge isn't a wall — it's the whole experience of the house.

Le Corbusier — the brise-soleil (the sun-breaker)

Working in hot climates (India, North Africa), Le Corbusier turned the facade into a deep, permeable brise-soleil — a built-in sun-breaker of fins and recesses that shades the wall while air and light still pass. It's the modern, architectural version of the screen: shading made into the building's very form, not bolted on.

The vernacular screen — mashrabiya & jali

Long before either, hot-climate builders perfected the permeable wall: the Arab mashrabiya and the Indian jali — carved lattices that cut glare, give privacy, and cool the air that passes through them. The lesson: a wall doesn't have to choose between open and closed. It can be both at once.

In the tropics the best room in the house is often the one that is only half a room — roofed, but open to the air.
— the veranda principle (the spirit of Bawa)

Tier 3 · Apply Layer your edge Your fourth artefact — one edge of your massing drawn as a gradient.

Hands-on. Take your chosen massing (Lesson 3) and pick its most important edge — usually the SW breeze face or the hot west face. Draw it in section (with a little plan) as a gradient of enclosure, naming every layer:

  1. Start outside — garden or court.
  2. Add a veranda / loggia under the roof's overhang (depth ~2 m or more).
  3. Add a screen / brise-soleil / louvre layer where you must cut sun or want privacy — especially on the west.
  4. Then the enclosed room.
  5. On the far side, open again to a courtyard or a second veranda.

For each layer, mark what it does to sun (caught? filtered?), breeze (passes through?), rain (kept off?) and privacy. Make at least one layer operable (shutters or louvres) so the edge can be tuned. Then show how the gradient resolves the old tension: the breeze face stays open and screened, while the west face gets a deeper veranda plus brise-soleil.

What you've made: your building's edge drawn as a gradient of enclosure — verandas, screens and courts doing the climate work the wall used to do alone. You now hold all four moves: climate → roof → massing → edge. Lesson 5 pulls them into one or two whole-form parti concepts.
