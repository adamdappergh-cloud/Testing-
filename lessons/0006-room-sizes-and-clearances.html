Lesson 5  ·  Residential Space Planning

Room Sizes & Clearances

How big a room really needs to be — and why bigger isn't the answer

This is the last lesson in the toolkit before the capstone. You've learned to read, zone, move through, and light a plan; now — how big should the rooms actually be? The Core gives you the clearances that decide whether a room works. The Deeper tier brings in three masters with a surprising message: the right size is set by the human body and how you'll use the room — not by how many square metres you can afford.

Tier 1 · Core A room is its furniture plus the space to move Usable size = the things in the room + the clearances around them.

Here's the shift that makes you dangerous in a meeting: a room's real size isn't a number you pick off a wish-list. It's the furniture you'll actually use, plus the clearances to move around it. A "big" room with a badly placed doorway can be unusable; a modest room with the right clearances works beautifully. So you don't ask "is 4×4 m enough?" — you draw the furniture and the gaps, and see.

The clearances worth memorising (everyday residential figures):1

WhereClearanceWhy
Main walkway / hall≥ 900 mmPass comfortably; 1050 mm+ is better
Behind a dining chair700–900 mmPull the chair out and squeeze past
Side & foot of a bed~ 600 mmMake the bed; walk around it
Kitchen work aisle1000–1200 mmBend, open doors; 1200 mm if two cooks
In front of a sofa~ 450 mmReach the coffee table; step through
The room isn't "the size of the table." It's the table, the chairs, and a pull-out-and-pass band on every side. Shrink the band and the room stops working.

So the method is always the same: draw the real furniture to scale, then add the clearance around it. What's left over is the actual room you need — often smaller than people guess, sometimes larger.

Your Core win. Pick one room on your plan and draw its real furniture to scale — the actual bed, the actual table and chairs. Then add the clearance bands from the table above. Does it fit with room to move? Is it tight in one spot? Or is there a lake of dead floor you're paying to build and heat?

You can stop here and already be ahead. Drawing furniture-plus-clearance is exactly how professionals size a room, and it will catch most "it looked big on the plan but we can't move in it" problems. When you want the masters' deeper take — that good size is matched to the body and the use, not maximised — read on.
Tier 2 · Deeper Size to the human, not to the number Le Corbusier, Alexander and Susanka: the right size is set by the body, a usable threshold, and what you'll actually live in.

The measure is the body (Le Corbusier)

Le Corbusier spent years building the Modulor — a system of proportions derived from the human body (a standing figure, an arm raised) woven together with the golden ratio. His aim: rooms, doorways and furniture sized so they fit the human frame and feel harmonious, in the old Vitruvian tradition of giving architecture human measure. The takeaway for you: the yardstick for a room is the body that will use it — reach, stride, sightline, the height you raise a hand to — not a round number that sounds generous.

Below a threshold, a space dies (Alexander)

Christopher Alexander found that dimensions have thresholds. His Pattern 167, Six-Foot Balcony, is blunt: "Balconies and porches which are less than six feet deep are hardly ever used."2 Below roughly 1.8 m, you can't pull up two chairs and face each other — so nobody goes out there. The same logic haunts skinny "study nooks," token window seats, and 2.4 m "dining" ends that no real table fits. When you see one, ask: is this past the threshold where it will actually get used, or is it a line on a drawing?

Alexander 167: a balcony too shallow to seat two people facing each other never gets used. Dimension decides whether a space lives.

Build better, not bigger (Susanka)

Sarah Susanka's The Not So Big House turns the instinct to enlarge on its head. Most homes waste money and life on rooms that are too big and rarely used — the formal living room nobody sits in. Her argument: reapportion square footage out of unused size and into the rooms you use every day, and into quality — built-ins, good light, fine detail. A right-sized room you live in beats a grand one you don't.3

Not So Big living… leaves excess square footage behind in favour of rooms and spaces that are used every day.

Put the three together and you have a sharp test for any room your architect proposes: is it sized to the body (Le Corbusier), past the threshold where it gets used (Alexander), and no bigger than it needs to be, with the savings spent where you live (Susanka)?

A related Alexander note worth carrying: vary your ceiling heights. A home where every ceiling is the same height feels flat; lower ceilings over intimate spaces and higher ones over shared rooms give the compression-and-release you met in Lesson 3.

Tier 3 · Apply Right-size a room Test one room against the body, the threshold, and "not so big."

Take a room on your plan and run the full test:

  1. Fit — draw the real furniture to scale and add the clearances. Does it work, or is it tight?
  2. Threshold — any nook, balcony, or "second" space too small to actually be used (Alexander 167)?
  3. Shape — any odd leftover or pinched corner that will just collect clutter?
  4. Not so big — is there a room that's large but rarely used, whose space (and budget) could move into one you'll live in daily (Susanka)?
