Lesson 1  ·  Residential Space Planning

Reading a Floor Plan

From decoding the symbols to reading what a space will feel like

This lesson comes in two tiers. The Core teaches you to decode a plan — scale, the symbols, and how to move through it. That alone will change how you sit in an architect's meeting. The Deeper tier, grounded in two masters — Steen Eiler Rasmussen and Frank Lloyd Wright — teaches you to read what a plan will feel like to live in. Take the Core today; come back for the depth when you're ready.

Tier 1 · Core Decode the plan Scale, the five symbols, and how to walk a plan in your head.

The core idea

A floor plan is a slice through the building at about chest height, looking straight down. Imagine cutting the house horizontally — removing the top half — and photographing what's left from above. You see the walls, doorways, windows, and stairs, but not the ceiling or roof.

Everything on a floor plan is drawn to scale — a fixed ratio between the drawing and real life. A typical residential scale is 1:50, meaning 1 centimetre on paper equals 50 centimetres in the building. When you see a room on paper that looks narrow, it probably is narrow. Scale keeps the drawing honest.1

First question to ask at any meeting: "What scale is this drawn at?" A 1:200 sketch is for big-picture layout decisions. A 1:50 drawing is for checking whether your furniture fits.

The five symbols you must know

Walls Drawn as thick solid lines or filled rectangles. Exterior walls are thicker than interior partitions. The thickness tells you something: exterior walls insulate; interior walls just divide.
Door A gap in the wall, with a thin rectangle (the door leaf) and a curved arc showing which way it swings. The arc is the most important part — it tells you how much space the door eats when open. A door swinging into a room needs about 90 cm of clear floor.
Window A break in the wall crossed by thin lines representing the glass. Notice where windows are — they tell you where natural light and views will be. A room with windows on one wall only gets light from one direction.
Stairs A series of parallel lines (the treads) with an arrow and "UP" or "DN". Where stairs land upstairs is just as important as where they start — they eat space on both floors. A stair taking the centre of a plan is a common problem.
North arrow Always find this first. It tells you which way the plan is oriented, so you can work out where the sun comes from. In the northern hemisphere, south-facing rooms get the most sun. A living room facing north will be permanently dim.1

How to "walk" through a plan

When you look at a floor plan, don't just scan it visually — move through it mentally. Start at the front door. Ask yourself:

Where do I arrive? What do I see first? To get to the kitchen, do I walk through the living room? If I'm in bed at night and need the bathroom, do I pass through a room where guests might be?

This mental walk surfaces problems that don't show up when you're just looking at shapes. Good layouts feel inevitable when you walk them. Bad ones have moments where you think "wait, that's strange."

The most common layout mistake in residential design: the corridor house — a long hallway with rooms off each side. It works in hotels. It feels institutional in a home. Ask your architect how circulation is handled.

3. You find the north arrow and see the main living area faces south. In the northern hemisphere, what does this tell you?

Your Core win. Next time you're handed a floor plan, do this before anything else:

  1. Find the north arrow and orient the plan in your head.
  2. Find the front door and mentally walk to the main living areas.
  3. Check the scale — is this a sketch or a precise drawing?
  4. Look at every door arc: are any swinging into awkward places?
You can stop here and already be ahead. Decoding scale, the symbols, and the mental walk is enough to ask useful questions in any meeting. When you want to read what a plan will feel like — not just what's where — carry on to the Deeper tier.
Tier 2 · Deeper Read the spatial intent From the masters: a plan is a description of an experience, not just a set of walls.

You can now decode a plan. But the masters would say you're still only reading the notation — not hearing the music. The next step is to read what the plan will feel like to inhabit.

A plan is a description of cavities

The Danish architect Steen Eiler Rasmussen, in Experiencing Architecture, taught that we don't really experience walls — we experience the cavities they carve out: the rooms, the volumes of air we move through. Architecture, he insisted, is something you experience with the whole body, not just look at.

So when you read a plan, flip your eye. The black lines are only the mould; the white spaces between them are the rooms you'll actually live in. Trained architects flick between the two readings — walls, then voids — almost without noticing.

Same plan, two readings. Architects design the rooms (right) but draw the walls (left). Learn to see the voids.

And those cavities are shaped above all by light and sound — the subjects of Rasmussen's most-loved chapters. As you read a plan, ask where the daylight enters (which walls hold windows, and which way they face — you learned orientation in the Core) and how sound will behave: tall, hard, open rooms ring; small, soft ones hush.

Flow versus the box

Frank Lloyd Wright spent a career on what he called "the destruction of the box." Where older homes were boxes-within-a-box — each room a sealed cell off a corridor — Wright dissolved the walls so spaces flow into one another. That is the open plan.

You can see the choice directly on a plan: are the rooms separate boxes joined by doors, or do living, dining and kitchen open into one continuous space? Each has a feel. Boxes give privacy and quiet; the open plan gives light, connection, and a sense of generosity. Neither is "right" — but now you can name what you're looking at, and say which you want where.

Wright also gave the home a centre: the hearth. The fireplace — "the heart of the home" — anchored his open plans, a fixed warm point the living spaces gathered around. So when you read a plan, look for its anchor. What does this home revolve around — a hearth, a kitchen island, a view, a stair? A home with no clear centre often feels restless to live in.

The three-question read. At each main space on the plan, ask: Light — where does it come from, and which way does it face? Openness — is this a sealed box or does it flow into the next space? Anchor — what does the room gather around?

Tier 3 · Apply Experience a room Read your own plan for feel, not just facts.

Pick the main living space on your plan and "experience" it on paper. Write three short sentences:

  1. Light — where it comes from, and which way the windows face.
  2. Openness — is it an enclosed box, or does it open into the next space?
  3. Anchor — what the room gathers around.

Bring those three sentences to your architect. You'll be talking about how the home will feel — the conversation that produces a home you love, not just one that works on paper.
