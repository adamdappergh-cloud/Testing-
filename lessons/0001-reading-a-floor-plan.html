Reading a Floor Plan

What you're looking at, and how to see it

The core idea

A floor plan is a slice through the building at about chest height, looking straight down. Imagine cutting the house horizontally — removing the top half — and photographing what's left from above. You see the walls, doorways, windows, and stairs, but not the ceiling or roof.

Everything on a floor plan is drawn to scale — a fixed ratio between the drawing and real life. A typical residential scale is 1:50, meaning 1 centimetre on paper equals 50 centimetres in the building. When you see a room on paper that looks narrow, it probably is narrow. Scale keeps the drawing honest.1

First question to ask at any meeting: "What scale is this drawn at?" A 1:200 sketch is for big-picture layout decisions. A 1:50 drawing is for checking whether your furniture fits.

The five symbols you must know

Walls Drawn as thick solid lines or filled rectangles. Exterior walls are thicker than interior partition walls. The thickness tells you something: exterior walls insulate; interior walls just divide.
Door A gap in the wall, with a thin rectangle (the door leaf) and a curved arc showing which way it swings. The arc is the most important part — it tells you how much space the door eats when open. A door that swings into a room needs 90cm of clear floor.
Window A break in the wall crossed by thin lines representing the glass. Notice where windows are — they tell you where natural light and views will be. A room with windows on one wall only gets light from one direction.
Stairs A series of parallel lines (the treads) with an arrow and "UP" or "DN". Where stairs land upstairs is just as important as where they start — they eat space on both floors. A stair taking up the centre of a floor plan is a common problem.
North arrow Always find this first. It tells you which direction the plan is oriented, which means you can work out where the sun comes from. In the northern hemisphere, south-facing rooms get the most sun. A living room facing north will be permanently dim.1

How to "walk" through a plan

When you look at a floor plan, don't just scan it visually — move through it mentally. Start at the front door. Ask yourself:

Where do I arrive? What do I see first? To get to the kitchen, do I walk through the living room? If I'm in bed at night and need the bathroom, do I pass through a room where guests might be?

This mental walk surfaces problems that don't show up when you're just looking at shapes. Good layouts feel inevitable when you walk them. Bad ones have moments where you think "wait, that's strange."

The most common layout mistake in residential design: the corridor house. A long hallway with rooms off each side. It works in hotels. It feels institutional in a home. Ask your architect how circulation is handled.

Quick knowledge check

Three questions — answer from memory

1. A floor plan drawn at 1:100 shows a bedroom that measures 3cm × 4cm on paper. How large is the room in real life?

3 m × 4 m in real life
1.5 m × 2 m in real life
0.3 m × 0.4 m in real life

2. You see a door arc swinging into a room. What does that tell you about the space behind the door?

The arc is only a decorative drawing convention
The wall on that side is load-bearing here
That floor space must stay clear for the swing
The room draws its natural light from there

3. You find the north arrow and see the main living area faces south. In the northern hemisphere, what does this tell you?

It will be cold and shaded for most months
It faces the street rather than the back garden
It gets the most daylight through the whole day
Its light depends on how big the windows are

Your win from this lesson

Next time you're handed a floor plan, do this before anything else:

  1. Find the north arrow and orient the plan in your head
  2. Find the front door and mentally walk to the main living areas
  3. Check the scale — is this a sketch or a precise drawing?
  4. Look at every door arc: are any swinging into awkward places?

That's it. That's enough to start asking useful questions.
