Lesson 3  ·  Residential Space Planning

Moving Through a Home

Circulation — from keeping the paths short to designing the journey

In Lesson 2 you grouped the rooms into zones. Circulation is the connective tissue that threads them together — the entry, the halls, the stairs, and the routes you take across rooms. It's the most overlooked part of a plan and the easiest to get wrong. The Core gives you the practical rules; the Deeper tier shows how the masters turn the path itself into the best part of the house.

Tier 1 · Core The rules of good circulation Short, generous, and never through a room it shouldn't be.

Circulation is the floor you travel but don't furnish — and it's easy to ignore precisely because nothing "happens" there. Yet you walk it every day, and you pay to build every square metre of it. Four rules catch most problems:

1. Keep the daily routes short. The trips you make dozens of times a day — kitchen to dining, bed to bathroom, car to kitchen with the shopping — should be short and direct. A few extra steps on a daily route is a tax you pay forever.

2. Don't spend floor on dead corridors. A long hall lined with doors is floor you pay for but never live in. The less plan you give to pure corridor, the more you give to rooms.

3. Never route through a room to reach another. You met this in zoning: the path to a bedroom shouldn't cross the living room. Circulation should run between rooms, not through the ones people are using.

4. Light and widen the paths you keep. A passage with a window at the end, or daylight along it, stops feeling like a tunnel.

And in a warm climate, let part of the path go outside. A covered veranda, a breezeway, or a route across a shaded courtyard connects rooms without enclosing floor — and unlike a dead indoor corridor, an open-air link catches the breeze and becomes somewhere to linger, not just pass through.

The grey hall on the left is floor you pay for but never live in. On the right, rooms open into one another and the path (blue) loops through the spaces you actually use.
The classic warning sign, which you first met in Lesson 1: the corridor house — a long spine hallway with rooms off each side. It's efficient for a hotel, where strangers need private doors. In a home it feels institutional, and it quietly eats a tenth of your floor.

a staircase set against an exterior outside wall

Your Core win. On your plan, trace — with a pen — every route you'd walk in a normal day: door to kitchen, kitchen to dining, bed to bath, sofa to back garden. Then shade any stretch that is pure corridor, doing nothing but connecting. Ask your architect: can the shaded area shrink, and does any route cross a room it shouldn't?

You can stop here and already be ahead. Short routes, little dead corridor, and no path through a room people are using — that's most of good circulation. When you want to see how the masters turn the path into the best part of the house, carry on.
Tier 2 · Deeper The path is the architecture Alexander, Le Corbusier and Wright treat circulation not as leftover space, but as the journey the house is built around.

Let the rooms be the route (Alexander)

Christopher Alexander's Pattern 131, The Flow Through Rooms, goes further than "keep corridors short" — it says to avoid them almost entirely. Arrange the common rooms in a chain or loop, so you walk from room to room rather than down a hall. A loop that passes through all the main rooms, he writes, "establishes an enormous feeling of generosity."1 In a warm climate the loop naturally spills outdoors — a veranda or shaded courtyard becomes one of the rooms the path flows through, which is just how Geoffrey Bawa's tropical houses dissolve the line between inside and out.

And when a passage truly is unavoidable, Pattern 132, Short Passages, says don't make it a tunnel — make it a room: short, wide, daylit, even furnished, "with carpets, bookshelves, easy chairs." The ideal is a one-sided hall lined with windows. A corridor only feels grim when it's narrow, long, and dark; fix those three and it becomes a place, not a duct.

Circulation as choreography (Le Corbusier)

Le Corbusier gave the idea a name: the promenade architecturale — the architectural promenade. A building, he argued, is revealed by moving through it along a deliberately composed path, a sequence of framed views and shifting light. At his Villa Savoye a ramp uncoils through the house, turning the simple act of going upstairs into the building's main event. Circulation here isn't the space between the architecture — it is the architecture.

You won't have a Villa Savoye ramp, but the lesson scales down: a good path should reveal the home, not just deliver you to rooms. Where does your plan offer a view as you arrive? Does anything unfold as you move, or do you simply pop out into each room?

Compression and release (Wright)

Frank Lloyd Wright — whose "destruction of the box" you met in Lesson 1 — designed the feeling of the journey through compression and release: a low, narrow, dim entry that opens into a tall, bright living space.2 The squeeze makes the release land; the living room feels larger and brighter than its dimensions alone, simply because of what you passed through to reach it. (His Unity Temple is the textbook case.)

Tier 3 · Apply Walk your promenade Read your plan's main route as a designed journey.

Take your plan and walk its main route in your head — front door → living → kitchen → main bedroom — and annotate it:

  1. Length — is the daily route short, or are you trekking? Any pure dead corridor?
  2. Flow — do rooms connect directly (Alexander), or are they beads on a hallway?
  3. Reveal — does anything unfold as you move — a view, a widening, more light (Le Corbusier)?
  4. Compression — is there a low, modest threshold that could open into the bright main space (Wright)?

Bring the annotated walk to your architect. Talking about the route, not just the rooms, is exactly the move that separates an active client from a passive one.
