Your Plan Through the Toolkit

The whole course, applied to your Ground Floor Plan — and an honest critique

This puts your real plan (the Preliminary Concept, 22 May 2026) through all five lessons at once. It's a concept drawing, so a few things — exact window positions, eaves, ceiling heights — can't be read off it and are flagged as "confirm." Everything else is on the table, and since you asked for a firm critique, this doesn't pull punches: the issues are ranked by how much they'd cost you in daily life if they reach construction unchanged.

Orientation key — reading the north arrow as drawn (it tilts only slightly): top = North, bottom = South, left = West, right = East. For Pokuase (~6°N): high overhead sun, hot low sun from the west each afternoon, and your cooling breeze is the south-west monsoon (from the bottom-left). Harmattan blows from the NE in Dec–Feb.

The verdict

A plan with good tropical instincts at its edges and a climate problem at its core. The outdoor rooms, gardens and service buffers around the perimeter are genuinely well judged. But two things aren't details — they're about the massing and the orientation, which is exactly why they're worth raising now, while it's still a concept and cheap to change: (1) the building is too deep to breathe in the middle, so its centre is dark and airless, and (2) the family's whole sleeping wing is lined up along the hot western wall. Both are fixable. Neither is a finish or a fitting — they're the bones.

Credit where it's due

Before the critique — these are real strengths, and you should defend them:
  • Outdoor cooking area (N) — keeps cooking heat and smoke out of the house. Textbook hot-climate move.
  • Terrace + Garden + Porch on the south — a shaded outdoor room placed right where it catches the SW breeze (Bawa's "the veranda is the real room").
  • Vegetable gardens on the west — the right place to grow shade against the afternoon sun (if you use them that way).
  • Car port, store and solar on the east — service mass soaking the morning sun.
  • A clear central anchor and a sound privacy gradient — guest near the entry, family deep, each bedroom with its own bath.

The critique, by severity

🔴 Major = about the bones (massing/orientation) · 🟠 Moderate = worth reworking · 🔵 Minor = confirm or tidy

MajorThe core can't breathe

The Library (14 m²) and Foyer sit dead in the middle of a ~13 m-deep plan with no external wall — so no daylight and no cross-ventilation. In hot-humid air that's a dark room you light artificially all day and have to cool mechanically: the exact opposite of the passive house the rest of the plan reaches for. Worse, that windowless Library is larger than your guest bedroom — a lot of prime floor spent on a room that can't be comfortable. This breaks Alexander's most-cited rule, Light on Two Sides of Every Room (Pattern 159).

Ask: "Can the Library take an external wall, or can we open a roof-lit core / clerestory over the centre so it gets daylight and lets hot air out? And is a 14 m² internal library really our priority for that space?"

MajorThe whole sleeping wing is on the hot west — and the master is in the worst corner

Master (SW), Kids (W) and Mother-in-law (NW) are all strung along the western wall, which takes the fierce afternoon sun. West walls store that heat and re-radiate it through the evening and night — precisely when bedrooms are occupied. Your master (24 m²) sits in the SW corner, hit by both the west and south sun: the most-used private room in the hottest spot in the house. The SW breeze is a genuine point in its favour for night cooling, but a breeze does not beat an unshaded, heat-soaked wall. Meanwhile the gentler east — better morning light, easy to buffer — is handed to the car port and a guest.

Ask: "What shades the west bedroom walls in the afternoon — deep eaves, screens, the vegetable-garden trees? And could the family bedrooms and the guest/car-port swap sides so we sleep on the cooler east?"

MajorThe massing is too deep to ventilate

At roughly 13 m deep, the plan is a thick block, and the middle is marooned from any breeze (the symptom is the dead core above). Hot-climate houses work best close to one room deep, so every room can have openings on two sides and the breeze passes clean through (Alexander 159; Bawa's dissolved, breathing plans). A fat plan forces you onto air-conditioning — which quietly undoes the solar setup and the outdoor kitchen.

Ask: "Can we thin the plan, or cut a courtyard / light-well into the centre, so the middle rooms get light and a through-draught instead of relying on AC?"

ModerateThe elder's room is in the noisiest corner

Mother-in-law's bedroom (NW, 17 m²) is wrapped by the kitchen, pantry, utility room, outdoor cooking and clothes-drying area — the busiest, most clattering quarter of the house. An older relative usually wants the calmest room; this is among the loudest, and it's poorly buffered (Lesson 2's acoustic + circulatory privacy).

Ask: "Can we buffer the mother-in-law's suite from the kitchen/utility noise — or move it to a quieter side?"

ModerateThe wet rooms are scattered — gather the plumbing

There are five WC/baths across four parts of the plan, including two stacked by the mother-in-law's room. Scattered wet rooms mean long, branching pipe runs, more cost, and more walls you can't easily change later. Kahn's lesson: gather the servant spaces with intent rather than sprinkling them.

Ask: "Can bathrooms be paired back-to-back on shared plumbing walls, and do we really need two beside the mother-in-law's room?"

ModerateThe entrance is small and ambiguous

A 2 m² porch and 3 m² foyer is a pinched welcome for a ~300 m² home, and it isn't clear whether visitors arrive at the south porch or the east car port. A small foyer can work as Wright's compression-into-release if it deliberately opens into the bright central space — but right now the front-door hierarchy reads by accident, not design.

Ask: "Which is the front door, and does the arrival sequence — approach, threshold, release into the living space — read clearly and feel generous?"

MinorConfirm-and-tidy list

  • Porch (2 m²) is below Alexander's usable threshold (Pattern 167) — fine, because the Terrace (14 m²) is your real outdoor room. Just don't expect to sit on the porch.
  • Covered route, car port → entry — confirm it's sheltered for the rains.
  • Kitchen (N) and the central G.W.C. — confirm real ventilation/extract (cooking heat; a windowless internal WC).
  • ~20 m² of corridor (9 + 8 + foyer) — check each stretch earns its keep rather than being floor you pay for but never live in.
  • Kids bedroom (19 m², two beds) — generous; worth asking whether it should be splittable later.

Areas only tell half the story (a 24 m² room that's long and thin can still fail), so where the short dimension decides it, I've said so. So on clearances, most rooms pass — the bedrooms, kitchen and ensuite are well-sized, even generous. The handful to pin down: the guest bedroom's short dimension, the dining-chair pull-out against the through-route, the 3 m² utility and foyer, the WC door swings, and the car-port count. And the one room that's the right size but the wrong room — the windowless Library.

The conversation to have

If you raise nothing else, raise these — in order:

  1. "How do the middle rooms get light and air?" — push for a courtyard/light-well or a roof-lit, clerestory core. (the deepest fix)
  2. "Can we sleep on the cooler east, and what shades the west walls?"
  3. "Can the plan be thinner so rooms cross-ventilate without AC?"
  4. "Can the mother-in-law's suite be quieter, and the bathrooms gathered onto shared walls?"
  5. "Which is the front door — and does arriving feel deliberate?"

None of this means starting over. The perimeter ideas are good; the work is to open the middle and turn the bedrooms off the west. Get those two right and the rest of the plan is already most of the way to a home that's comfortable without fighting the climate.
