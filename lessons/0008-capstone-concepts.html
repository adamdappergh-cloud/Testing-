Two Ways to Fix the Bones

Concept directions for the dead core and the deep footprint — to take to your architect

The capstone critique landed on two structural problems: the landlocked, windowless core, and a plan too deep to cross-ventilate. Here are two different ways to attack them. These are sketches to argue from, not drawings to build — schematic and not to scale. One keeps a single storey and opens the middle; the other goes partly upstairs and shrinks the whole footprint.

Concept A · The central courtyard

Wrap the house around a void

Instead of a fat block with dead rooms in the middle, the house becomes a ring of rooms around an open, planted courtyard. The space that's now your windowless Library becomes a shaded outdoor room open to the sky — and every room that used to be landlocked now faces it.

Concept A: rooms ring a planted courtyard. The old dead centre becomes the lung — daylight and the SW breeze reach every room, and warm air vents up out of the court.
Why it works (the masters agree):
  • Light on two sides everywhere — every room now has an outer window and a courtyard window (Alexander 159). The Library problem disappears.
  • It cools itself — a shaded, planted courtyard with water is Hassan Fathy's central cooling device: it shades the heart of the house, and warm air rising out of the court draws fresh air through the rooms (stack-effect ventilation).
  • The veranda ring becomes the circulation — you move room-to-room under shade around the court, indoors and out blurring together (Bawa). No more dark internal corridors.
  • The west bedrooms get a second, shaded source of light from the courtyard, so they rely less on hot west windows.
Trade-offs to weigh: a courtyard uses footprint — it keeps the building roughly as large (or a touch larger) on the ground, so it suits a generous plot (which yours is — you have garden and vegetable-garden room to give). It also needs thought on drainage and rain (a tropical downpour fills a courtyard fast) and a little upkeep on the planting.

Concept B · Lift the bedrooms, shrink the footprint

Part single-storey, part two

Keep the public rooms (living, dining, kitchen) and the elder's suite on the ground, and stack the other bedrooms onto a partial upper floor. The same accommodation now sits on a much smaller footprint — so the plan can be thin (one room deep), every room cross-ventilates, and you free up the plot.

Concept B: a tall single-storey living wing beside a two-storey sleeping block. Same rooms, far less ground covered — and the upstairs catches more breeze.
Why it works:
  • Smaller footprint, thinner plan — fewer square metres on the ground means the plan can be one-room-deep, so every room cross-ventilates and gets daylight. The deep-block problem is gone by design.
  • The breeze is better upstairs — air moves faster above ground-level walls, trees and fences, so first-floor bedrooms catch the SW monsoon more cleanly.
  • More plot, less roof-baked floor — you free garden and setback, and stacking puts less floor directly under the hot roof.
  • Varied heights add life — a tall, single-storey living wing against the two-storey block gives the ceiling-height variation and compression/release you met in Lessons 3 and 5.
Trade-offs to weigh: a stair costs floor on both levels and needs placing well; heat rises, so the upstairs bedrooms need real defences — a well-insulated, ventilated roof, high ceilings, deep eaves and cross-breeze. And keep the mother-in-law's suite (and ideally a guest room) on the ground floor for accessibility — stairs and elders don't mix.

And they combine. The strongest answer for a hot climate is often both — a smaller, two-level plan wrapped around a courtyard: the court lights and cools the middle while the upper floor keeps the footprint tight. It's more to design and build, but it's exactly the lineage Fathy and Bawa worked in.
