Zoning, in Depth

The privacy gradient, and the diagram architects draw before any rooms

In Lesson 2 you learned the three zones and three rules for arranging them. Architects don't really think in rules, though — they think with one underlying principle and one simple tool. Learn both and you can sketch a workable layout yourself, and say precisely why a plan feels right or wrong.

The principle: a privacy gradient

The deepest idea in residential zoning comes from Christopher Alexander's A Pattern Language — Pattern 127, the Intimacy Gradient. It says: don't merely separate public from private — arrange every space in a sequence, so that privacy increases gradually as you move deeper into the home.

Unless the spaces… are arranged in a sequence which corresponds to their degrees of privateness, visits made by strangers, friends, guests, clients, family, will always be a little awkward.
— Christopher Alexander, Pattern 127

Picture who travels how far in: a courier stops at the door; a guest settles in the living room; a close friend ends up leaning on the kitchen counter; only family crosses into the bedrooms. A good plan lets each person stop at the right depth without ever feeling they've trespassed — or been held at arm's length.

The intimacy gradient: each step inward is more private than the last, and each visitor stops at the depth that fits.

This reframes the buffering idea from last lesson. A buffer isn't just a wall between bedroom and lounge — it's a rung on the gradient. The entry hall, the stair, the family room: each one eases you a step further from public toward private.

Privacy comes in three flavours, and good plans stack them: visual (can they see in?), acoustic (can they hear through?), and circulatory (does anyone pass through?). A closed door solves sight; only distance and a buffer room solve sound and traffic.

The tool: bubble & adjacency diagrams

Before a single room is drawn, architects sketch the whole plan as bubbles. It's how they apply the gradient without committing to walls. The workflow:1

  1. List every space — and be specific (a pantry is not a coat closet; they want different neighbours).
  2. Size each roughly: large, medium, or small.
  3. Build an adjacency matrix — for every pair of rooms, mark how close they want to be.
  4. Draw bubbles sized by importance, connect the ones that must be adjacent, and redraw it two or three times.
  5. Turn bubbles into rectangles on the site, and you have the beginnings of a floor plan.

The adjacency matrix is the part you can use today. Read it like a distance chart: find two rooms, read the cell where they meet. Here is one for six common rooms:

Notice the matrix quietly encodes the gradient: kitchen touches dining, garage touches kitchen (groceries), the en-suite touches its bedroom — but the bedrooms are marked ✕ against kitchen, dining, and living. The private zone is held at a distance on every front.

Quick knowledge check

Three questions — answer from memory

1. The privacy gradient says a good home should arrange its spaces so that…

privacy increases as you move from the entry inward
every room sits the same distance from the front door
the kitchen is always the deepest and quietest room
bedrooms open directly off the main entrance hallway

2. On an adjacency matrix, a "keep these apart" mark fits which pair best?

the kitchen and the dining room beside it
the main bedroom and the family living room
the garage and the kitchen, for the groceries
the en-suite bathroom and its own bedroom

3. In the architect's workflow, the bubble diagram comes…

before the floor plan, after the adjacency matrix
after the floor plan, once the rooms are sized
before listing any of the home's needed spaces
only after the construction drawings are signed

Your win from this lesson

Do this for your own home, on paper — it's exactly what your architect did, and doing it yourself lets you check their work:

  1. List your rooms and size each one (large / medium / small).
  2. Fill in a blank adjacency matrix — the cheat-sheet has one ready to print.
  3. Sketch bubbles and draw a line between every ● pair.
  4. Walk the gradient: from the front door inward, does privacy rise steadily — or does a bedroom appear too early?

Then bring the bubble sketch to your next meeting. "Here's the adjacency I'm after" is one of the most useful things a client can hand an architect — it turns vague preferences into a spec they can design against.
