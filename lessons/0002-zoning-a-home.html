Zoning a Home

From the three zones, to the gradient and the diagram architects draw before any rooms

In Lesson 1 you learned to read a plan. Now you learn to judge one. This lesson has two tiers: the Core gives you the working tool — the three zones — that's enough to spot most bad layouts. The Deeper tier gives you the principle and the diagram the professionals actually use, grounded in Christopher Alexander and Louis Kahn. Take the Core today; come back for the depth when you're ready.

Tier 1 · Core The three zones The fastest lens for telling a good layout from a bad one.

Before worrying about any single room, architects group rooms into three zones by how they're used:

Public
Where you gather and receive guests. Living, dining, kitchen, guest WC — and in a warm climate this zone often spills outdoors onto a veranda or shaded courtyard, which can become the real gathering space. Should sit close to the front entry and flow together. The kitchen is the in-between case — a working space gone social — so it usually bridges public and service.
Private
Where you rest and retreat. Bedrooms, en-suites, a study. These "should be placed away from high-traffic zones… in a quieter part of the home, providing a sense of security and seclusion."
Service
Where the house does its work. Laundry, utility, pantry, store, carport or garage. They support the other zones and are happiest tucked near where they're used — laundry near the bedrooms, pantry and store near the kitchen.

What good zoning looks like

Public Service Private Circulation / buffer
Enter into the public zone. The kitchen (service) sits beside the dining (public). A hall buffers the private zone, which is the deepest, quietest part of the plan.

The thing beginners miss: zoning isn't about which rooms exist — it's about how the zones relate. Three relationships do most of the work:

1. Buffer the private zone. Never open a bedroom door straight into the living room. Put a hall, a stair, or a service room between the noise and the rest.

2. Keep traffic out of zones it doesn't belong in. You shouldn't walk through the dining room to reach a bedroom, or cut across the kitchen's work area to reach the garden.

3. Place service where it serves. The pantry and store want to be a few steps from the kitchen; the laundry wants to be near the bedrooms it serves.

The single test that catches most bad layouts: "At night, to get from my bed to the front door, what rooms do I cross?" If it routes you through a sleeping child's room or a guest's space, the zoning is wrong.

Three mistakes to watch for

The most common residential zoning errors are: making people pass through living areas to reach bedrooms, allowing cross-traffic through the kitchen work zone, and dead-end hallways that waste space.1 All three are easy to spot once you've coloured the zones in.

Your Core win: take the latest plan from your architect and, with three coloured pens, colour every room public, private, or service. Check the private zone is buffered, trace the path from the front door to each bedroom, and note whether service sits where it serves. If anything's off, you can already say it precisely: "This bedroom isn't buffered from the living space — can we put the hall between them?"

You can stop here and already be ahead. The three zones plus the night-walk test will let you spot most layout problems and ask sharper questions. When you want to understand why these rules hold — and the diagram architects use to design with them — carry on to the Deeper tier below.
Tier 2 · Deeper The gradient, the hierarchy, and the diagram The principle beneath the rules (Alexander), a sharper lens (Kahn), and the tool architects draw first.

The principle: a privacy gradient

The deepest idea in zoning comes from Christopher Alexander's A Pattern Language — Pattern 127, the Intimacy Gradient. Don't merely separate public from private — arrange every space in a sequence, so privacy increases gradually as you move deeper into the home.

Unless the spaces… are arranged in a sequence which corresponds to their degrees of privateness, visits made by strangers, friends, guests, clients, family, will always be a little awkward.
— Christopher Alexander, Pattern 127

Picture who travels how far in: a courier stops at the door; a guest settles in the living room; a close friend ends up at the kitchen counter; only family crosses into the bedrooms. A good plan lets each person stop at the right depth without feeling they've trespassed — or been held at arm's length.

The intimacy gradient: each step inward is more private than the last, and each visitor stops at the depth that fits.

This reframes the buffering rule from the Core tier. A buffer isn't just a wall between bedroom and lounge — it's a rung on the gradient. The entry hall, the stair, the family room: each eases you a step further from public toward private.

Privacy comes in three flavours, and good plans stack them: visual (can they see in?), acoustic (can they hear through?), and circulatory (does anyone pass through?). A closed door solves sight; only distance and a buffer room solve sound and traffic.

A sharper lens: served & servant (Kahn)

Louis Kahn cut the plan a second way. Every space is either served — where life happens (living room, bedroom, study) — or servant — the support that makes it possible (corridors, bathrooms, kitchens, stairs, storage, mechanical).2 His point: don't scatter the servant spaces as leftovers. Gather them with intent, and the served spaces become clear, calm, and well-shaped.

Use it as a test alongside the gradient: run a finger over the plan and label each space served or servant. If the servant spaces are deliberately clustered (a service core, a stack of bathrooms, a run of storage), the plan has hierarchy. If they're sprinkled randomly between the rooms you actually live in, the architect filled in rooms rather than organising them.

The tool: bubble & adjacency diagrams

Before a single room is drawn, architects sketch the plan as bubbles. It's how they apply the gradient and the served/servant split without committing to walls. The workflow:3

  1. List every space — be specific (a pantry is not a storeroom).
  2. Size each roughly: large, medium, or small.
  3. Build an adjacency matrix — for every pair of rooms, mark how close they want to be.
  4. Draw bubbles sized by importance, connect the ones that must be adjacent, and redraw it two or three times.
  5. Turn bubbles into rectangles on the site, and you have the beginnings of a floor plan.

The adjacency matrix is the part you can use today. Read it like a distance chart: find two rooms, read the cell where they meet.

Tier 3 · Apply Your own plan Turn the theory into a sketch you can hand your architect.
  1. List your rooms and size each one (large / medium / small).
  2. Fill in a blank adjacency matrix — the cheat-sheet has one ready to print.
  3. Sketch bubbles and draw a line between every ● pair.
  4. Label each space served or servant — are the servant spaces gathered, or scattered?
  5. Walk the gradient: from the front door inward, does privacy rise steadily — or does a bedroom appear too early?

Bring the bubble sketch to your next meeting. "Here's the adjacency I'm after" is one of the most useful things a client can hand an architect — it turns vague preferences into a spec they can design against.
