Lesson 4  ·  Residential Space Planning

Light & Orientation

Letting the sun set the plan

You can read a plan, zone it, and move through it. Now the factor that should arguably drive all of those: the sun. Where light falls decides which rooms belong where — and it's the one thing about your site you cannot change later. The Core gives you the orientation rules; the Deeper tier shows how the masters treated light not as a feature but as the very material of architecture.

Tier 1 · Core Orient to the sun Put each room where the light it needs actually falls.

In Lesson 1 you learned to find the north arrow. Here's why it's the most important mark on the drawing. In the northern hemisphere the sun rises in the east, swings across the south, and sets in the west — it is never in the north. Each direction gives a different light:1

  • South — the prize. Sun all day, and the most consistent. Reserve it for the rooms you live in by day: living, kitchen, dining.
  • East — morning. Gentle early light. Good for breakfast spaces and bedrooms, where waking with light is a pleasure.
  • West — evening, with care. Low, hot, glaring afternoon sun. Fine for rooms used in the evening, but a big west window cooks a room and dazzles you. Shade it.
  • North — even and cool. No direct sun, but soft, steady, shadowless light. Perfect for the spaces that don't need warmth: garage, laundry, utility, bathrooms, stairs — and, prized by artists, studios.

So the rule is simple: place rooms by when you use them and the light they want. Living spaces chase the sun; service and circulation can take the north and the shade. One free bonus — a house whose long axis runs east–west presents a big face to the south, which makes good orientation almost automatic.

Give the sunny south to the rooms you use by day; send the service spaces to the cool north.
This is where two ideas can collide. The privacy gradient (Lesson 2) may want bedrooms at the quiet back of the house — but the sun may be at the front. A good plan resolves the clash deliberately; a weak one just picks one and forgets the other. Spotting that tension is a sharp question for your architect.

Checkpoint

Three questions — answer from memory

the daytime rooms — living, kitchen, dining
the garage, the laundry and the storage

the cold buffer spaces and the long halls

2. Why treat a big west-facing window with caution?

It never receives any direct sun at all
Low evening sun brings glare and overheating
It makes the room colder right through winter

3. North-facing rooms (northern hemisphere) suit…

the main living room used right through the day
the breakfast room that wants warm morning sun
service spaces and studios wanting steady light
the warmest, sunniest gathering spot in the home

Your Core win. On your plan, find north and sketch the sun's path — east, up over the south, down to the west. Then check: do the rooms you use by day sit on the sunny side? Do bedrooms catch the morning? Is there a wall of west-facing glass about to cook the living room of an afternoon? Three questions, and you'll already be ahead of most clients.

You can stop here and already be ahead. Orienting rooms to the sun by when you use them is most of the win. When you want to treat light the way the masters did — as the material the architecture is actually made of — carry on.
Tier 2 · Deeper Light is the material Kahn, Le Corbusier, Alexander and Rasmussen on light as the thing architecture is made of — and one rule that makes or breaks a room.

A room needs light to be a room (Kahn)

No one put it more starkly than Louis Kahn: "A room is not a room without natural light."2 For Kahn, daylight wasn't lighting — it was what gave a space life and mood, and that mood is never still: a room "is a different room every second of the day" as the light moves. His most quoted line captures the awe: "The sun never knew how great it was until it hit the side of a building."

The lesson for a client: don't just ask "is there a window?" Ask what the light does — which direction it comes from, what surfaces it grazes, how it will change from morning to evening and summer to winter.

Light reveals form (Le Corbusier)

Le Corbusier opened Towards a New Architecture with it: "Architecture is the masterly, correct and magnificent play of masses brought together in light. Our eyes are made to see forms in light; light and shade reveal these forms."2 Flat, even, shadowless light flattens a space; light raking across a wall or pouring from one side gives a room depth and shape. Shadow is not the enemy of light — it's the half that makes light visible.

The rule that makes or breaks a room (Alexander)

Christopher Alexander's Pattern 159, Light on Two Sides of Every Room, is blunt: this pattern, he writes, "perhaps more than any other single pattern, determines the success or failure of a room." People gravitate to rooms lit from two sides and quietly abandon rooms lit from one. A single-sided room fights you with glare by the window and gloom across from it; light from two sides softens the shadows, so you can even read the expressions on people's faces.2

Alexander 159: windows on two sides of a room wash out the harsh shadows of a single-sided room — and people feel the difference.

Steen Eiler Rasmussen (whose cavities you met in Lesson 1) devotes whole chapters of Experiencing Architecture to exactly this — how the quality of light, direct or reflected, the play of light and shade, is what you actually feel in a room. The through-line from all four masters: light isn't poured into finished rooms. The rooms are shaped around the light.

Tier 3 · Apply Trace the sun on your plan Read your home for light, room by room.

Take your plan, find north, and draw the sun's arc (E → S → W). Then, for each main room, note:

  1. Aspect — which way does it face, and is that right for when you use it?
  2. Two sides — how many walls have windows? Mark every room lit from only one side (Alexander 159).
  3. Trouble — any west wall of glass to shade, or any key room left in the north gloom?
  4. Clash — where do the sun and the privacy gradient pull against each other, and how does the plan settle it?

That annotated plan is a genuinely expert thing to bring to a meeting — you'll be discussing the one site condition the architect can't undo.
