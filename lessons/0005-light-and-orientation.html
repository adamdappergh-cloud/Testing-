Lesson 4  ·  Residential Space Planning

Light & Orientation

Working with the sun — for your climate, not someone else's

You can read a plan, zone it, and move through it. Now the factor that should drive much of it: the sun. But here's what most guides get wrong — how you orient a home depends entirely on its climate. The masters' ideas about light as a material hold everywhere; the thermal strategy flips depending on where you build. This lesson gives you the principle, then the rules for your build: Pokuase, Ghana — about 6° north of the equator, in the hot, humid tropics.

Tier 1 · Core Orient to your climate's sun Cool climates chase the sun. Hot ones reject it and chase the breeze.

In Lesson 1 you learned to find the north arrow. Why it matters depends on your latitude. Two regimes:

Cool / temperate climates (for contrast)

Where winters are cold, the sun is a welcome guest. You capture it: put daytime living rooms on the equator-facing side (south, in the northern hemisphere), and shade only the harsh summer west. This is the advice in most house-design books — and it's wrong for you.

Hot tropics — your case (Pokuase, ~6°N)

Near the equator the sun rides high overhead almost all year (it even swings slightly north of vertical from about May to August). Heat and glare — not cold — are the enemy. So the goal inverts: keep the sun out, and let the air move.1

  • Beware east, and above all west. Low morning (E) and low, hot afternoon (W) sun drives deep under any roof and bakes a room. West is the worst orientation in the tropics. Keep east/west glazing small and screened; park buffers — garage, store, utility — on the west to soak the heat.
  • North and south are the easy faces. High overhead sun is simple to block with horizontal shade — eaves, overhangs, verandas. (Low E/W sun needs vertical shade — screens, fins, planting.)
  • Cross-ventilation is comfort. In humid air, moving air is everything. Give living spaces openings on two opposite sides so a breeze flows through; a long, thin plan ventilates best.
  • Catch the prevailing breeze. On the coast near Accra the prevailing wind is the south-west monsoon most of the year — so south / south-west openings catch the cooling air. (The dry, dusty Harmattan blows from the north-east in Dec–Feb — a lesser, seasonal concern.)
  • Make shaded outdoor rooms. Verandas, the porch, the terrace, the outdoor kitchen — shaded outdoor space is prime living space here, not a leftover.
  • Light roofs and walls, lighter construction — pale finishes and materials that shed heat rather than store it.
High midday sun on the north/south is easy to block with an eave. The low west sun slides underneath — which is why west glazing overheats and needs screening, not just an overhang.
Cross-ventilation: openings on two opposite sides let the SW breeze sweep through. A room open on one side only stays stuffy in humid heat.
On your plan already: your open living/dining and the porch, terrace, garden and outdoor kitchen all sit to the south — well placed to catch the south-west breeze, and the high south sun is easy to shade. The things to watch (we'll do this properly in the capstone): any west-facing habitable rooms baking in the afternoon, and whether each room can be opened on two sides for a through-breeze.

Tier 2 · Deeper Light is the material — and so are shade and air Kahn, Le Corbusier and Alexander on light everywhere; Fathy and Bawa on designing with a hot-climate sun.

A room needs light to be a room (Kahn)

Louis Kahn put it most starkly: "A room is not a room without natural light."2 Daylight isn't lighting — it's what gives a space life and mood, and that mood is never still: a room "is a different room every second of the day." Le Corbusier agreed that light is what makes architecture visible at all: "Architecture is the masterly, correct and magnificent play of masses brought together in light." The lesson is universal — ask not just "is there a window?" but what the light does.

The rule that makes or breaks a room (Alexander)

Christopher Alexander's Pattern 159, Light on Two Sides of Every Room, holds anywhere: people gravitate to rooms lit from two sides and abandon rooms lit from one, because two-sided light cancels the harsh glare-and-gloom of a single window.2 In the tropics it pays a second dividend: openings on two sides give you light and the cross-breeze. The same move solves two problems at once.

Designing with a hot-climate sun (Fathy & Bawa)

Two masters made the tropics their life's work. The Egyptian architect Hassan Fathy revived hot-climate vernacular wisdom: orient and shade against the sun, draw air through with wind-catchers (the malqaf) and shaded courtyards, and let heavy or pale walls and planting do the cooling — "work with nature," not against it.3

And the Sri Lankan master Geoffrey Bawa, father of tropical modernism, dissolved the wall between inside and out — deep verandas, courtyards, overhanging eaves, water — so the house simply breathes. His summary is the thesis of this whole lesson:

Comfort in the tropics is mostly a question of shade, cross-ventilation and water — and only lastly a question of machinery.
— Geoffrey Bawa

So near the equator the through-line from all the masters holds, with a twist: light is still the material — but you sculpt it with shade, and you pair it with moving air. Kahn's daylight, made liveable by Bawa's shade and breeze.

Tier 3 · Apply Trace the sun and the breeze on your plan Read your home for a hot, humid climate.

Take your plan, find north, and remember: high overhead sun, hot low sun from east and west, prevailing breeze from the south-west. Then, room by room:

  1. West watch — any habitable room facing west without heavy shade or a buffer? That's your overheating risk.
  2. Shade — do the north/south openings have eaves or verandas for the high sun?
  3. Breeze — can the south-west wind enter and leave each living space (openings on two sides)?
  4. Outdoor rooms — are the shaded outdoor spaces (veranda, porch, terrace) where you'll actually live in the heat of the day?

That's exactly the lens we'll use on your whole plan in the capstone.
